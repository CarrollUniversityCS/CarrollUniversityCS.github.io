Election-night benchmarks

On election night, a county’s vote count means little on its own. Is D+40 good news or bad news for Democrats in Milwaukee County? This page gives every county a benchmark: the result it would show if the whole state ends where you choose. Compare a place’s final result with its benchmark to see whether it ran ahead of or behind the pace.

Not a forecast. A benchmark says what a place would need to show for a given statewide result. It does not say who will win. Use it only on complete counts; partial counts can mislead.

How to read it in 30 seconds

  1. Choose a statewide result.Drag the slider, or tap Tied, D+3 or R+3. “D+3” means Democrats lead the state by 3 points.
  2. Find a place.Tap a county on the map, or pick it from the list. Switch to municipalities or districts if you prefer.
  3. Compare.Enter what the place shows. The page says whether the gap is ordinary, using the place’s own past misses.

All margins use the two-party vote: only Democratic and Republican votes are counted, so third-party and write-in votes are left out. Margin = Democratic share minus Republican share.

How accurate has this been?

Before the election, we ran the method on past elections as if they had not happened yet, using only earlier results. That is a backtest. Here is what it found.

Counties, overall

Weighted by votes, benchmarks were off by about 1.9 points on average in four tests on 2022 and 2024 elections, and 3.1 points across nine tests back to 2016, when the method was tested on harder, older elections.

One county at a time

Small counties swing more. The middle county’s typical miss over nine tests is about 5 points, and the widest is 10. That is why every place shows its own typical miss.

Cities, towns and districts

Municipalities missed by 3.2 and 2.5 points in the two tests, so they carry a higher variance label. District tables are drafts resting on two tests only.

Our pass line, set before the election: a vote-weighted average miss of 4.0 points or less across all 72 counties. After the election we will report the result whether it flatters us or not.

For reporters and researchers

Use it carefully

  • Compare final county counts, not partial returns. The order ballots are counted varies by municipality.
  • Say “ahead of its benchmark,” not “winning.” A benchmark is not a call.
  • Check the place’s own typical miss before calling a gap unusual.
  • Use the district layer only with the draft label.

Quick facts to cite

  • Method: past results (2018–2024, recent elections weighted more) re-centred on the statewide result you choose.

Method and checks

Short version: for each place we measure how much more Democratic or Republican it has voted than the state. We give recent elections more weight and add a quarter of the place’s recent trend. Then we shift everything so the benchmarks average exactly to the statewide result you choose.

How each benchmark is built
  1. Lean. For seven past statewide contests (2018 to 2024), take the place’s margin minus the state’s. Average those leans, weighting 2022 and 2024 most.
  2. Trend. Add 0.25 times how much the lean has moved between the earliest and the recent contests. This version was chosen by a rule written before any test was run.
  3. Size. Each place’s share of the state vote comes from the 2018 and 2022 midterms.
  4. Re-centre. Shift so that vote-weighted benchmarks average exactly to the statewide result. Because this is a simple shift, a D+3 state moves every place by 3 points from the tied table.

Districts are different: their table compares county parts with the district’s own result, so that layer has its own slider.

How it was checked
  • A second program, written without seeing the first, reproduces 87,170 published numbers: vote counts exactly and everything else to one part in a million. Both followed one written specification, so this catches mistakes in the code, not in the method itself.
  • A fresh rebuild from the raw files ran 20 steps and 144 tests on 2026-10-30 and changed 0 published files.
  • Every county table is checked against the Wisconsin Elections Commission’s printed statewide total.
  • Municipality bubbles sit at the vote-weighted centre of each municipality’s 2022 wards. They show where the votes are, not the legal boundary.
  • The source code is not public yet.
What this method cannot do
  • It does not know about this year’s candidates, money, or events. It only extends past patterns.
  • It does not model who turns out. A county can break from its history for good reasons.
  • Older tests (2016 to 2020) were much harder than recent ones; that is why the all-nine-tests numbers are larger.
  • Four backtests on two election years is a short record. Treat the ranges as rough.

The method, the county, municipality and district tables, the pass line, and the list of public forecasts to compare with were fixed and fingerprinted on October 30, 2026, at 4:03 PM CDT. Anything changed later would be published as a dated correction, never as a quiet edit.

Glossary and common questions

Margin
Democratic share minus Republican share of the two-party vote. D+3.0 means Democrats lead by 3.0 points; R+3.0 means Republicans do.
Two-party vote
Only Democratic and Republican votes. Other candidates are left out so the margin stays simple.
Benchmark
The final margin a place would show if the statewide result were the one you chose, based on how it voted in the past.
Lean
How much more Democratic or Republican a place is than the state, in points.
Typical miss
How far a place’s final result landed from its benchmark in past tests (a vote-weighted root-mean-square, so big misses count more).
Usual range
The benchmark plus and minus the typical miss. Roughly two in three past results fell inside it.
Backtest
Running the method on a past election using only the results before it.
Frozen
Fingerprinted and published so it cannot be changed unnoticed.
Draft
Published but tested on too few elections to trust as much as the county table.
Is this a prediction?

No. It never says who will win. It says what a place would need to show for the state to land at a particular result.

Why only complete counts?

Which ballots are counted first depends on how each municipality organizes its count. A county that is 60% reported can look very different from its final result.

What does “less predictable” mean?

A place whose typical miss is 5 points or more. Its range is wide, so a big gap there tells you less.

Why are only two districts shown, and why draft?

WI-1 and WI-3 are the two districts this project covers. Each was tested on only two past elections (2022 and 2024), so we label them drafts.

Do the municipality bubbles show city limits?

No. Each bubble sits at the vote-weighted centre of the municipality’s wards, and its size shows the share of the state vote.
