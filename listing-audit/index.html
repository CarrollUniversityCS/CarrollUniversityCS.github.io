Are home photos edited without telling buyers?

Many home listings now show photos that were changed with software or AI: furniture added to an empty room, or a grey sky swapped for a blue one. A new Wisconsin law says sellers' agents must disclose these changes when they could mislead buyers, starting January 1, 2027. This study checks how often listings do so, before and after that date.

Study in progress · no results yet

The short version

The question

Do listings say when their photos have been changed, and does that change once the law takes effect?

The method

Randomly chosen Milwaukee-area listings are saved as they appear publicly. Each photo and description is checked for a disclosure.

The goal

A plain count of how common disclosure is, before and after the law, published as totals only. No individual home, agent or brokerage is named.

What counts as an edited photo

Some changes are routine. Others change what a room or a property actually looks like. The study focuses on the second kind.

Not counted: brightening a dark photo, straightening a crooked one, or cropping. These make a photo look better but do not change what the property looks like.

How the study works

Five steps, the same for every listing, from the first sample to the published totals.

  1. Pick at randomListings are chosen by a random draw, so the sample is not hand-picked.
  2. Save what is publicPhotos and the written description are saved as they appear on the public listing.
  3. Look for disclosuresEach photo is checked for a label, and each description for wording such as "photos are digitally altered."
  4. Compare the periodsThe share of listings with a disclosure is compared before and after January 1, 2027.
  5. Publish totalsResults are released as overall counts only. No home, agent or brokerage is named.

What the study will show

We do not know the answer yet. There are three broad possibilities. Each one would tell us something different.

Disclosure rises

More listings say when their photos were changed after the law takes effect. The change could be due to the law, or to other things happening at the same time.

Little changes

Disclosure stays about the same. Little change would suggest disclosure is not moving with the date.

Disclosure falls

Fewer listings disclose. This could reflect changes in how portals display photos, not just changes in behaviour.

Results not yet available The first counts will go on this page when they are ready, with no numbers shown before then.

What this study can and cannot tell you

It can tell you

  • How common photo disclosure is in a random sample of Milwaukee-area home listings.
  • Whether disclosure changes around the date the law takes effect.
  • How often listings carry a disclosure label or wording that the study can count.

It cannot tell you

  • Whether any single listing broke the law. That is for the regulator.
  • That the law caused any change. Other things may shift at the same time.
  • Edits that carry no disclosure. The study cannot count these directly.

Timeline

  1. Late 2026: pilot and first sampleTesting the method on a small set of listings and drawing the first random sample.
  2. January 1, 2027: the law takes effectFrom this date, the disclosure requirement applies.
  3. Early 2027: first reportA short public report of the first totals, planned for early 2027.
  4. Later in 2027: follow-up samplesRepeat samples to see whether the pattern holds over time.

Who is doing this, and how data is handled

ListingAudit is run by Jack Forden in the Department of Computer Science at Carroll University, Waukesha, Wisconsin. It is research by faculty, not an official statement of the university.

We keep only what the count needs. We do not publish listing photos, names, or addresses, and results appear only as totals.
